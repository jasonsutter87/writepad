Every writer who revises ends up with a folder like this:

```
chapter3.docx
chapter3-v2.docx
chapter3-tighter.docx
chapter3-final.docx
chapter3-final-FINAL.docx
chapter3-final-FINAL-use-this-one.docx
```

It happens because revision is experiment. You want to try the tighter
opening *without losing the old one*, in case the old one was better. The
files are a way of keeping your nerve.

The trouble is that they're blind. You can't see what changed between v2 and
"tighter", you can't put the tighter opening in the book and the old ending
alongside it, and three months later you can't remember which one you liked.

## What you actually need

Strip it down and a revising writer needs four things:

1. **Keep every version of a scene,** side by side, in one place.
2. **See exactly what changed** between any two of them.
3. **Choose one** to go in the book, without deleting the others.
4. **Save combinations.** "The 2024 draft, but with the new opening" is a
   whole-book choice, not a chapter choice.

## Versions per scene, not per book

The key shift is to version each scene (or chapter) on its own instead of
copying the whole manuscript. A book is made of pages, and each page can have
several takes. When you want to try something, you **fork** the page: copy the
current take and rewrite the copy. The original stays exactly as it was.

In WRITEpad, each page shows its takes as tabs above the text. One is starred:
that's the **live** take, the one that goes into the book. Fork, rewrite, and
when the new one wins, press **★ Make live**. The old one is still there.

## Compare by onion skin

Seeing two versions side by side is slow. Your eye jumps back and forth and
misses the small changes, which are the ones that matter in a line edit.

**Onion skin** lays one version over the other. Words you added are tinted;
words the other version had are drawn as faint ghosts in place. A slider fades
the ghosts in and out. You read the new version and see the old one under it,
and a line like "+124 words, −49 words vs tighter opening" tells you which way
the revision went.

## Cuts: whole-book choices

Once each page has takes, the book itself becomes a choice: which pages, in
what order, with which take of each. WRITEpad calls a saved choice a **cut**,
like a film editor's cut.

- **Jacobs 1902**: every page in order, the original wording.
- **Modern pass**: the same, but with the revised take of "The first wish".
- **Cold open**: opens on the knock at the door, then goes back to the start.
- **Short**: the same story with two scenes left out.

Click a cut and the book switches to it: the order changes, the starred takes
change, and pages the cut doesn't use are set aside (not deleted). Export (text or MP3)
and the word count follow the cut you're on.

## A simple routine

1. Before a revision pass, **save a cut** of the book as it stands. Now you can
   always get back.
2. **Fork** each page you rework. Name the take for the idea ("tighter",
   "Herbert's POV"), not the date.
3. Read the new take with the old one **onion-skinned** underneath.
4. **Make live** the winners. Leave the losers. They cost nothing.
5. **Save a cut** at the end of the pass, so the book has a name for each
   stage of its life.

You'll never need `final-FINAL-use-this-one.docx` again.
