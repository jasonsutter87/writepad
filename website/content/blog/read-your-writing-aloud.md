Every editing guide says it: **read your work aloud.** It's the one piece of
advice that every writer agrees on, and the one most of us skip, because it's
slow, it's a little embarrassing, and our voice gives out by chapter four.

It's worth finding a way to do it anyway. Here's why it works, and how to get
the benefit without reading 90,000 words out loud yourself.

## Why your ear catches what your eyes miss

When you reread your own writing silently, you read what you *meant*, not what
you *wrote*. Your brain knows the sentence and fills it in. A missing "the", a
doubled word, a sentence that changed halfway through a revision: your eye
slides right over them.

Speech can't skip. Every word has to be said, in order, at the speed of a
voice. That slows you down enough to notice:

- **Rhythm.** Three sentences in a row with the same shape. A paragraph that
  never lets the reader breathe.
- **Repetition.** The same word twice in a line, which is invisible on the
  page and glaring out loud.
- **Dialogue that nobody would say.** If it sounds stiff spoken, it is stiff.
- **Tangled sentences.** If the reader (or the voice) runs out of breath
  before the verb, the sentence is too long.
- **Typos that spellcheck can't see.** "Form" for "from". "Their" for "there".

## Let something else do the reading

Reading aloud yourself has a flaw: you still know what you meant, and you'll
perform it. You'll give a flat line the emphasis it needs, and the problem
disappears.

A text-to-speech voice doesn't know what you meant. It reads exactly what's
there, with no help. That makes it a harsher and more useful listener. When it
stumbles, a reader will too.

## What to listen for in each pass

1. **First listen: just listen.** Don't stop. Note where your attention
   drifted. That's where the pacing sagged.
2. **Second listen: follow along.** Watch the words as they're read and stop at
   anything that made you wince.
3. **For dialogue: give characters their own voices.** A table read with a
   different voice per character shows you fast when two characters sound the
   same on the page.

## How WRITEpad does it

WRITEpad has a **Listen** button at the top of the window. It reads from the
cursor (or just the selection) and highlights each sentence as it goes, so you
can follow along. **Esc** stops it.

- **It's local.** The voices run on your own computer. Your manuscript isn't
  uploaded anywhere to be read.
- **Pick a voice and a speed.** Slower for line edits, faster for a pacing
  pass.
- **Table reads for scripts and dialogue.** Screenplays are read with a voice
  per character, and you choose who plays whom.
- **Teach it names.** Fantasy names and odd spellings can be given a
  pronunciation once, and every voice uses it from then on.
- **A/B listen.** When you have two versions of a page, it reads one and then
  the other, so you can hear which revision works.

You can also export the book as MP3 chapters, which is a fine way to hear your
draft on a walk, away from the desk where you wrote it.

Your reader will hear your book in their head. Hear it first.
