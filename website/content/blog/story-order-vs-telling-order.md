Every story has two orders.

There's the order things **happened**: the soldier buys the paw in India, the
Whites wish on it, the son dies at the factory, the knock comes at the door.
And there's the order you **tell** them in. Maybe you open on the knock and
reach back for the rest.

Narrative theory calls these *fabula* (the story as it happened) and *syuzhet*
(the story as told). You don't need the Russian to use the idea, but you do
need to keep the two apart, because almost every continuity error in a
nonlinear book comes from mixing them up.

## Where it goes wrong

- A character mentions an event the reader hasn't been shown yet, but which
  has happened in the story. That's fine, if you meant it.
- A character mentions an event that *hasn't happened yet* in the story. That's
  a continuity error, and it hides well when the telling jumps around.
- Two narrators tell the same night, and one of them gets the order wrong.
- A flashback lands in chapter 12, but chapter 7 already relied on what the
  reader learns in it.

You can't see any of this in the manuscript alone, because the manuscript *is*
the telling order. You need the other order written down somewhere.

## Keep a timeline of events, not chapters

Write the story's events in the order they happened, each with a short name:
"Morris visits Laburnam Villa", "The first wish", "Herbert dies at Maw and
Meggins". Then link each event to the scene (or scenes) where the reader sees
it.

Now you can ask the useful question: **for each event, where is it told?** If
you draw the events down one side in story order and the scenes down the other
in telling order, the lines between them cross wherever you've told something
out of order. A straight book draws parallel lines. A flashback draws one line
cutting back across the others.

## How WRITEpad shows it

WRITEpad's **World** keeps characters, places, things and events as plain
notes in your vault. The **Timeline** lists the events in story order, each
linked to the scenes it happens in.

Then you can **read the timeline against a telling**: the manuscript as it
stands, or one of its saved cuts. Each event shows where it falls in that
telling, and events told earlier than their place in the story are flagged as
**told earlier**. A flowchart draws the two orders side by side, so the jumps
are lines you can see.

That last part matters most for books told more than one way. If you keep a
cut for each narrator (Matthew's account, Mark's account), you can read the
same timeline against each telling and see exactly where they diverge: which
events one narrator skips, which they move, and which they tell first.

## A routine for nonlinear drafts

1. **List events in story order** before you restructure, not after.
2. **Link each event to its scenes.** An event with no scene is something the
   reader never sees; make sure that's deliberate.
3. **Read the timeline against your current telling** after every structural
   change. Look at the "told earlier" flags: each one should be a choice.
4. **Save a cut before you move chapters around,** so you can compare the old
   telling with the new one.

Telling a story out of order is one of the strongest tools you have. Keeping
the real order written down is what lets you use it on purpose.
