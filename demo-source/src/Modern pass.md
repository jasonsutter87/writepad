## The first wish

Mr. White took the paw from his pocket and turned it over. “I don’t know what to wish for, and that’s a fact,” he said slowly. “Seems to me I’ve got all I want.”

“If you only paid off the house, you’d be quite happy, wouldn’t you?” said Herbert, with a hand on his shoulder. “Well, wish for two hundred pounds, then. That’ll just do it.”

His father, smiling at his own foolishness, held up the talisman, while his son, with a solemn face spoiled by a wink at his mother, sat down at the piano and struck a few grand chords.

“I wish for two hundred pounds,” said the old man clearly.

A crash from the piano greeted the words, cut off by a cry from the old man. His wife and son ran to him.

“It moved,” he cried, staring in disgust at the thing on the floor. “As I wished, it twisted in my hand like a snake.”

*It moved. It moved in my hand.*

“Well, I don’t see the money,” said his son, picking it up and setting it on the table, “and I bet I never will.”

“It must have been your fancy, father,” said his wife, watching him anxiously.

He shook his head. “Never mind. There’s no harm done, but it gave me a shock all the same.”

They sat by the fire again while the two men finished their pipes. Outside, the wind was higher than ever, and the old man jumped at the bang of a door upstairs. A heavy silence settled on all three, and lasted until the old couple went up to bed.

“I expect you’ll find the cash tied up in a big bag in the middle of your bed,” said Herbert as he said good-night, “and something horrible squatting on top of the wardrobe, watching you pocket your ill-gotten gains.”

He sat alone in the dark, watching the dying fire and seeing faces in it. The last face was so horrible, so like an ape’s, that he stared at it in amazement. It grew so vivid that, with an uneasy laugh, he felt on the table for a glass of water to throw over it. His hand closed on the monkey’s paw. With a little shiver he wiped his hand on his coat and went up to bed.
