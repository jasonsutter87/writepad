## The bell-row

I was nineteen the winter I went to St. Aldric's as under-sexton. Nobody had ever paid me to stand among the dead before, though I had stood among them often enough for nothing. A boy who buries a mother and a sister in one hard year finds the churchyard quieter than the house.

The churchyard lay along the top of the railway cutting. On the north side the stones leaned a little toward the line, as if they were listening for the trains. When the trains came, they came up out of the cutting with a roar that shook the frost off the yews.

Abel Crane had kept the place thirty years. He was a small, bent, careful man with a voice like a door that needs oiling, and he walked me round it on my first morning with his hands behind his back.

“This is the bell-row,” he said, stopping at the north wall.

There were eleven graves in it, and at the head of each, where a stone should have been, stood an iron post about as high as my hip. A little bell hung from a bracket at the top, no bigger than the bell on a shop door, green with weather and polished bright on one side where the wind kept turning it against the post. A cord ran down from each bell into a pipe, and the pipe ran down into the ground, and the grass grew close and neat around every one of them, as though somebody tended it with particular care.

“For them as is afraid of waking up,” said Abel. “Families pay. The cord goes down to the box and ties round the hand. If they wake, they pull, and the bell rings, and somebody comes and digs.”

“And has one ever rung?”

“Every night,” said Abel cheerfully, and spat. “The goods train at twenty to twelve. Shakes them all. You'll learn to sleep through it.”

“But if one really rang?”

He looked at me for a while.

“If one really rang,” he said, “you come and fetch me. You don't ever dig alone. That's the rule here. You fetch me.”

“Why not alone?”

“Because a man digging alone at night in a churchyard,” said Abel slowly, “gets to thinking, and a man who gets to thinking down a hole is no use to anybody, least of all to the party in the box.”

The twelfth grave in the bell-row was dug that afternoon, for a man named Fenn, a corn-chandler who had died of a fever in two days. His widow was a tall woman in good black who did not cry. When the service was over and the vicar had gone in out of the wind, she came and stood beside me while Abel threaded the cord down the pipe.

“You are the new man,” she said.

“Yes, ma'am.”

“He was always afraid of it,” she said, looking at the hole. “Of being shut in. When we were first married he would not have the bed-curtains drawn.”

She opened her purse and put a coin into my hand without looking at it, and I saw that it was gold.

“If it rings, you will go to him.”

“Yes, ma'am. I'll fetch Mr. Crane.”

“I did not ask you to fetch Mr. Crane,” said Mrs. Fenn. “I asked you to go to him.”

So I said that I would. I was nineteen, and the coin was gold, and I would have promised a great deal more than that.

## The quiet night

Abel was right about the trains. On my first night in the lodge by the gate I heard the goods train come up out of the cutting at twenty to twelve. With it, faint and thin and all together, like a shop door opening in another street, came the twelve bells of the bell-row. By the end of the week I slept through it. By the end of the month I could hear the train and not hear the bells at all.

But in that month I saw a thing I did not understand. Coming back late from the town one evening, I found Abel at the head of Fenn's grave on his knees. His sleeves were rolled up and his arm was down the pipe to the shoulder, working at something under the ground.

“What are you doing there?” I said.

He pulled his arm out quickly and wiped it on the grass. “Rat in the pipe,” he said. “Chokes the cord.” And he went off to his own cottage without another word.

Then in February it snowed for three days. The snow came down so thick and steady that the yews bent under it and the church tower went grey and then white and then disappeared altogether, and it drifted into the cutting until the line was closed and the signal-lamps stood up out of it like the masts of sunk ships. On the third night there were no trains at all.

It was the quiet that woke me. I lay listening to it, as you listen to a clock that has stopped. Then, quite close and quite clear, a single bell rang in the bell-row. It rang once, and stopped, and rang again three times, as if whoever pulled it had learned the trick of it.

I lit the lantern and went out, and the snow was over my boots at the first step and over my knees by the north wall. It lay smooth and blue over the twelve graves, without a mark on it anywhere, not a bird's foot, not a fox's, and over eleven of them the bells hung still with little caps of snow on them. The twelfth was bare. The snow had been shaken off it, and lay in a ring on the ground around the post, and there was no track leading to it but mine.

I ran for Abel. He came to his door in his shirt, and when I told him which bell, he laughed. Then he stopped laughing and put out a hand to the door-post, as if the floor had moved.

“It can't ring,” he said.

“I heard it. I saw it.”

“It can't ring. I tied it.”

“Tied it?”

“A foot under the turf,” he said. “Every one of them. Did you think I'd get up twelve times a night for the goods train? The families never look. I did Fenn's the night you saw me. Pulled the knot tight with these hands.” He held them up, and they were shaking. “Thirty years, Tom, and not one of them ever woke. Not one. They don't wake.”

“Then what rang it?”

He did not answer me. From the churchyard behind us, through the snow, the bell rang again.

## The cord

I took the spade from the shed.

“Tom,” said Abel behind me. “Tom. The rule.”

“I promised her.”

“You promised me first.”

“No,” I said. “You told me. That's not the same.”

He said nothing after that, but he put on his coat and came after me with the lantern, a long way behind.

The ground was soft under the snow, for it had not been filled a month, and I dug as I have never dug since, throwing the earth up black over the white until the hole was deeper than I was tall and the lantern above me was only a yellow smudge at the edge of the world. I do not know how long it was. Once the bell rang above my head, so close that I felt it in my teeth, and I heard Abel cry out and drop something. After that it did not ring again.

“Tom,” he called down. “Tom, are you there?”

“I'm here.”

“Then come up out of it. There's nothing down there wants you.”

When the spade struck wood, Abel came to the edge with the light. I found the pipe and followed it down with my hand to where the cord came out of it, and I felt for his knot.

There was no knot. The cord ran straight and whole from the bell into the box. It was pulled tight, as tight as a fiddle string, and when I touched it, it slackened in my hand.

“Leave it,” said Abel. “For God's sake, Tom, leave it be and fill it in.”

I got the lid up. I will say only that Fenn lay as he had been laid, a month dead, with his hands folded on his breast. The cord was wound twice round his right wrist, as Abel had wound it. And the knot that Abel had tied a foot under the turf lay loose in the dead man's fingers, untied, the way you would hold a thing you meant to give back.

“Is he...?” said Abel.

“He's dead,” I said. “He's been dead a month.”

Abel did not come down into the grave. He stood above me with the lantern shaking. Very quietly, he said, “I never meant no harm by it.” I do not know whether he was saying it to me.

He did not live out the winter. Before he died he asked the vicar to bury him in the bell-row, with a bell, and the vicar thought it a strange humility in an old man and allowed it. I threaded the cord down the pipe myself. I did not tie it off.

I have kept St. Aldric's forty years now. Long ago they electrified the line, and the trains go by in the night with only a hum. The bells do not ring for them any more. But sometimes, on the quietest nights, one of them rings, the one at the end of the row, and I lie in the lodge and listen to it.

I made Mrs. Fenn a promise, and I kept it. I made Abel Crane none.
