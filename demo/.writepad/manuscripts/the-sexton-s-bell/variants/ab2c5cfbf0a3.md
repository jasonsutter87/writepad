Abel was right about the trains. The first night I lay in the lodge by the gate and heard the goods train come up out of the cutting at twenty to twelve, and with it, faint and thin and all together, like a shop door opening in another street, the twelve bells of the bell-row. By the end of the week I slept through it. By the end of the month I could hear the train and not hear the bells at all.

But I saw a thing, in that month, that I did not understand. I came back late from the town one evening and found Abel at the head of Fenn's grave on his knees, with his sleeves rolled up and his arm down the pipe to the shoulder, working at something under the ground. When he heard the gate he pulled his arm out and wiped it on the grass and said that a rat had got into the pipe, and went off to his own cottage without another word.

Then in February it snowed for three days, and the snow drifted into the cutting until the line was closed, and on the third night there were no trains at all.

I remember that the quiet woke me. I lay listening to it, as you listen to a clock that has stopped, and then, quite close and quite clear, a single bell rang in the bell-row. It rang once, and stopped, and rang again, three times, as if whoever pulled it had learned the trick of it.

I lit the lantern and went out. The snow lay smooth and blue over the twelve graves, and over eleven of them the bells hung still with caps of snow on them. The twelfth was bare. The snow had been shaken off it.

I ran for Abel. He came to his door in his shirt, and when I told him which bell, he did a thing I have never forgotten: he laughed, and then he stopped laughing, and put out a hand to the door-post as if the floor had moved.

“It can't ring,” he said.

“I heard it. I saw it.”

“It can't ring,” he said again. “I tied it.” And then, because I only stared at him, it came out of him all at once: how he had tied off every cord in the bell-row a foot under the turf, where the families would never look, so that the trains should not get him out of bed on a fool's errand twelve times a night; how he had done Fenn's the evening I saw him, and pulled the knot tight with his own hands. “Thirty years,” he said, “and not one of them ever woke. Not one. They don't wake.”

From the churchyard behind me, through the snow, the bell rang again.
