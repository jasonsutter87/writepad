I was nineteen the winter I went to St. Aldric's as under-sexton. Nobody had ever paid me to stand among the dead before, though I had stood among them often enough for nothing. A boy who buries a mother and a sister in one hard year finds the churchyard quieter than the house.

The churchyard lay along the top of the railway cutting. On the north side the stones leaned a little toward the line, as if they were listening for the trains. When the trains came, they came up out of the cutting with a roar that shook the frost off the yews.

Abel Crane had kept the place thirty years. He was a small, bent, careful man with a voice like a door that needs oiling, and he walked me round it on my first morning with his hands behind his back.

“This is the bell-row,” he said, stopping at the north wall.

There were eleven graves in it, and at the head of each, where a stone should have been, stood an iron post about as high as my hip. A little bell hung from a bracket at the top, no bigger than the bell on a shop door, green with weather and polished bright on one side where the wind kept turning it against the post. A cord ran down from each bell into a pipe, and the pipe ran down into the ground, and the grass grew close and neat around every one of them, as though somebody tended it with particular care.

“For them as is afraid of waking up,” said Abel. “Families pay. The cord goes down to the box and ties round the hand. If they wake, they pull, and the bell rings, and somebody comes and digs.”

“And has one ever rung?”

“Every night,” said Abel cheerfully, and spat. “The goods train at twenty to twelve. Shakes them all. You'll learn to sleep through it.”

“But if one really rang?”

He looked at me for a while.

“If one really rang,” he said, “you come and fetch me. You don't ever dig alone. That's the rule here. You fetch me.”

“Why not alone?”

“Because a man digging alone at night in a churchyard,” said Abel slowly, “gets to thinking, and a man who gets to thinking down a hole is no use to anybody, least of all to the party in the box.”

The twelfth grave in the bell-row was dug that afternoon, for a man named Fenn, a corn-chandler who had died of a fever in two days. His widow was a tall woman in good black who did not cry. When the service was over and the vicar had gone in out of the wind, she came and stood beside me while Abel threaded the cord down the pipe.

“You are the new man,” she said.

“Yes, ma'am.”

“He was always afraid of it,” she said, looking at the hole. “Of being shut in. When we were first married he would not have the bed-curtains drawn.”

She opened her purse and put a coin into my hand without looking at it, and I saw that it was gold.

“If it rings, you will go to him.”

“Yes, ma'am. I'll fetch Mr. Crane.”

“I did not ask you to fetch Mr. Crane,” said Mrs. Fenn. “I asked you to go to him.”

So I said that I would. I was nineteen, and the coin was gold, and I would have promised a great deal more than that.
