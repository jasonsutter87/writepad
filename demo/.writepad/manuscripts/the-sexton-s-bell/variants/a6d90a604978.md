I took the spade from the shed.

“Tom,” said Abel behind me. “Tom. The rule.”

“I promised her.”

“You promised me first.”

“No,” I said. “You told me. That's not the same.”

He said nothing after that, but he put on his coat and came after me with the lantern, a long way behind.

The ground was soft under the snow, for it had not been filled a month, and I dug as I have never dug since, throwing the earth up black over the white until the hole was deeper than I was tall and the lantern above me was only a yellow smudge at the edge of the world. I do not know how long it was. Once the bell rang above my head, so close that I felt it in my teeth, and I heard Abel cry out and drop something. After that it did not ring again.

“Tom,” he called down. “Tom, are you there?”

“I'm here.”

“Then come up out of it. There's nothing down there wants you.”

When the spade struck wood, Abel came to the edge with the light. I found the pipe and followed it down with my hand to where the cord came out of it, and I felt for his knot.

There was no knot. The cord ran straight and whole from the bell into the box. It was pulled tight, as tight as a fiddle string, and when I touched it, it slackened in my hand.

“Leave it,” said Abel. “For God's sake, Tom, leave it be and fill it in.”

I got the lid up. I will say only that Fenn lay as he had been laid, a month dead, with his hands folded on his breast. The cord was wound twice round his right wrist, as Abel had wound it. And the knot that Abel had tied a foot under the turf lay loose in the dead man's fingers, untied, the way you would hold a thing you meant to give back.

“Is he...?” said Abel.

“He's dead,” I said. “He's been dead a month.”

Abel did not come down into the grave. He stood above me with the lantern shaking. Very quietly, he said, “I never meant no harm by it.” I do not know whether he was saying it to me.

He did not live out the winter. Before he died he asked the vicar to bury him in the bell-row, with a bell, and the vicar thought it a strange humility in an old man and allowed it. I threaded the cord down the pipe myself. I did not tie it off.

I have kept St. Aldric's forty years now. Long ago they electrified the line, and the trains go by in the night with only a hum. The bells do not ring for them any more. But sometimes, on the quietest nights, one of them rings, the one at the end of the row, and I lie in the lodge and listen to it.

I made Mrs. Fenn a promise, and I kept it. I made Abel Crane none.
