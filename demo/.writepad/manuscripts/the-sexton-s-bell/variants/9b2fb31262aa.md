Abel was right about the trains. On my first night in the lodge by the gate I heard the goods train come up out of the cutting at twenty to twelve. With it, faint and thin and all together, like a shop door opening in another street, came the twelve bells of the bell-row. By the end of the week I slept through it. By the end of the month I could hear the train and not hear the bells at all.

But in that month I saw a thing I did not understand. Coming back late from the town one evening, I found Abel at the head of Fenn's grave on his knees. His sleeves were rolled up and his arm was down the pipe to the shoulder, working at something under the ground.

“What are you doing there?” I said.

He pulled his arm out quickly and wiped it on the grass. “Rat in the pipe,” he said. “Chokes the cord.” And he went off to his own cottage without another word.

Then in February it snowed for three days. The snow came down so thick and steady that the yews bent under it and the church tower went grey and then white and then disappeared altogether, and it drifted into the cutting until the line was closed and the signal-lamps stood up out of it like the masts of sunk ships. On the third night there were no trains at all.

It was the quiet that woke me. I lay listening to it, as you listen to a clock that has stopped. Then, quite close and quite clear, a single bell rang in the bell-row. It rang once, and stopped, and rang again three times, as if whoever pulled it had learned the trick of it.

I lit the lantern and went out, and the snow was over my boots at the first step and over my knees by the north wall. It lay smooth and blue over the twelve graves, without a mark on it anywhere, not a bird's foot, not a fox's, and over eleven of them the bells hung still with little caps of snow on them. The twelfth was bare. The snow had been shaken off it, and lay in a ring on the ground around the post, and there was no track leading to it but mine.

I ran for Abel. He came to his door in his shirt, and when I told him which bell, he laughed. Then he stopped laughing and put out a hand to the door-post, as if the floor had moved.

“It can't ring,” he said.

“I heard it. I saw it.”

“It can't ring. I tied it.”

“Tied it?”

“A foot under the turf,” he said. “Every one of them. Did you think I'd get up twelve times a night for the goods train? The families never look. I did Fenn's the night you saw me. Pulled the knot tight with these hands.” He held them up, and they were shaking. “Thirty years, Tom, and not one of them ever woke. Not one. They don't wake.”

“Then what rang it?”

He did not answer me. From the churchyard behind us, through the snow, the bell rang again.
