I was nineteen the winter I went to St. Aldric's as under-sexton, and I had never before been paid to stand among the dead, though I had stood among them often enough for nothing, in the way of a boy who has buried a mother and a sister in one hard year and finds the churchyard quieter than the house. The churchyard lay along the top of the railway cutting, so that the stones on the north side leaned a little toward the line, as if they were listening for the trains, and the trains, when they came, came up out of the cutting with a roar that shook the frost off the yews.

Abel Crane had kept the place thirty years. He was a small, bent, careful man with a voice like a door that needs oiling, and he walked me round it on my first morning with his hands behind his back.

“This is the bell-row,” he said, stopping at the north wall.

There were eleven graves in it, and at the head of each, where a stone should have been, stood an iron post about as high as my hip, with a little bell hung from a bracket at the top. A cord ran down from each bell into a pipe, and the pipe ran down into the ground.

“For them as is afraid of waking up,” said Abel. “Families pay. The cord goes down to the box and ties round the hand. If they wake, they pull, and the bell rings, and somebody comes and digs.”

“And has one ever rung?”

“Every night,” said Abel, and laughed, and spat. “The goods train at twenty to twelve. Shakes them all. You'll learn to sleep through it.”

“But if one really rang?”

He looked at me for a while.

“If one really rang,” he said, “you come and fetch me. You don't ever dig alone. That's the rule here. You fetch me.”

The twelfth grave in the bell-row was dug that afternoon, for a man named Fenn, a corn-chandler who had died of a fever in two days. His widow was a tall woman in good black who did not cry, and when the service was over and the vicar had gone in out of the wind, she came and stood beside me while Abel threaded the cord down the pipe.

“You are the new man,” she said.

“Yes, ma'am.”

“He was always afraid of it,” she said, looking at the hole. “Of being shut in. When we were first married he would not have the bed-curtains drawn.” She opened her purse and put a coin into my hand without looking at it, and I saw that it was gold. “If it rings, you will go to him.”

“Yes, ma'am. I'll fetch Mr. Crane.”

“I did not ask you to fetch Mr. Crane,” said Mrs. Fenn. “I asked you to go to him.”

I said that I would. I was nineteen, and the coin was gold, and I would have promised a great deal more than that.
