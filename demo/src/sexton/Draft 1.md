## The bell-row

I was nineteen the winter I went to St. Aldric's as under-sexton, and I had never before been paid to stand among the dead, though I had stood among them often enough for nothing, in the way of a boy who has buried a mother and a sister in one hard year and finds the churchyard quieter than the house. The churchyard lay along the top of the railway cutting, so that the stones on the north side leaned a little toward the line, as if they were listening for the trains, and the trains, when they came, came up out of the cutting with a roar that shook the frost off the yews.

Abel Crane had kept the place thirty years. He was a small, bent, careful man with a voice like a door that needs oiling, and he walked me round it on my first morning with his hands behind his back.

“This is the bell-row,” he said, stopping at the north wall.

There were eleven graves in it, and at the head of each, where a stone should have been, stood an iron post about as high as my hip, with a little bell hung from a bracket at the top. A cord ran down from each bell into a pipe, and the pipe ran down into the ground.

“For them as is afraid of waking up,” said Abel. “Families pay. The cord goes down to the box and ties round the hand. If they wake, they pull, and the bell rings, and somebody comes and digs.”

“And has one ever rung?”

“Every night,” said Abel, and laughed, and spat. “The goods train at twenty to twelve. Shakes them all. You'll learn to sleep through it.”

“But if one really rang?”

He looked at me for a while.

“If one really rang,” he said, “you come and fetch me. You don't ever dig alone. That's the rule here. You fetch me.”

The twelfth grave in the bell-row was dug that afternoon, for a man named Fenn, a corn-chandler who had died of a fever in two days. His widow was a tall woman in good black who did not cry, and when the service was over and the vicar had gone in out of the wind, she came and stood beside me while Abel threaded the cord down the pipe.

“You are the new man,” she said.

“Yes, ma'am.”

“He was always afraid of it,” she said, looking at the hole. “Of being shut in. When we were first married he would not have the bed-curtains drawn.” She opened her purse and put a coin into my hand without looking at it, and I saw that it was gold. “If it rings, you will go to him.”

“Yes, ma'am. I'll fetch Mr. Crane.”

“I did not ask you to fetch Mr. Crane,” said Mrs. Fenn. “I asked you to go to him.”

I said that I would. I was nineteen, and the coin was gold, and I would have promised a great deal more than that.

## The quiet night

Abel was right about the trains. The first night I lay in the lodge by the gate and heard the goods train come up out of the cutting at twenty to twelve, and with it, faint and thin and all together, like a shop door opening in another street, the twelve bells of the bell-row. By the end of the week I slept through it. By the end of the month I could hear the train and not hear the bells at all.

But I saw a thing, in that month, that I did not understand. I came back late from the town one evening and found Abel at the head of Fenn's grave on his knees, with his sleeves rolled up and his arm down the pipe to the shoulder, working at something under the ground. When he heard the gate he pulled his arm out and wiped it on the grass and said that a rat had got into the pipe, and went off to his own cottage without another word.

Then in February it snowed for three days, and the snow drifted into the cutting until the line was closed, and on the third night there were no trains at all.

I remember that the quiet woke me. I lay listening to it, as you listen to a clock that has stopped, and then, quite close and quite clear, a single bell rang in the bell-row. It rang once, and stopped, and rang again, three times, as if whoever pulled it had learned the trick of it.

I lit the lantern and went out. The snow lay smooth and blue over the twelve graves, and over eleven of them the bells hung still with caps of snow on them. The twelfth was bare. The snow had been shaken off it.

I ran for Abel. He came to his door in his shirt, and when I told him which bell, he did a thing I have never forgotten: he laughed, and then he stopped laughing, and put out a hand to the door-post as if the floor had moved.

“It can't ring,” he said.

“I heard it. I saw it.”

“It can't ring,” he said again. “I tied it.” And then, because I only stared at him, it came out of him all at once: how he had tied off every cord in the bell-row a foot under the turf, where the families would never look, so that the trains should not get him out of bed on a fool's errand twelve times a night; how he had done Fenn's the evening I saw him, and pulled the knot tight with his own hands. “Thirty years,” he said, “and not one of them ever woke. Not one. They don't wake.”

From the churchyard behind me, through the snow, the bell rang again.

## The cord

I took the spade from the shed. Abel said my name, and then he said the rule, and then he said nothing at all, but he put on his coat and came after me with the lantern, a long way behind.

The ground was soft under the snow, for it had not been filled a month, and I dug as I have never dug since. I do not know how long it was. Once the bell rang above my head, so close that I felt it in my teeth, and after that it did not ring again. When the spade struck wood Abel came to the edge with the light, and I found the pipe and followed it down with my hand to where the cord came out of it, and I felt for his knot.

There was no knot. The cord ran straight and whole from the bell into the box, and it was pulled tight, as tight as a fiddle string, and when I touched it, it slackened in my hand.

I got the lid up. I will say only that Fenn lay as he had been laid, a month dead, with his hands folded on his breast. The cord was wound twice round his right wrist, as Abel had wound it. And the knot that Abel had tied a foot under the turf lay loose in the dead man's fingers, untied, the way you would hold a thing you meant to give back.

Abel did not come down into the grave. He stood above me with the lantern shaking, and I heard him say, very quietly, “I never meant no harm by it,” and I do not know whether he was saying it to me.

He did not live out the winter. He asked the vicar, before he died, to be buried in the bell-row, with a bell, and the vicar thought it a strange humility in an old man and allowed it. I threaded the cord down the pipe myself. I did not tie it off.

I have kept St. Aldric's forty years now. The line was electrified long ago, and the trains go by in the night with only a hum. The bells do not ring for them any more. But sometimes, on the quietest nights, one of them rings, the one at the end of the row, and I lie in the lodge and listen to it.

I made Mrs. Fenn a promise, and I kept it. I made Abel Crane none.
